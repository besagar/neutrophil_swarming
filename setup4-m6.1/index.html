Setup 4c — Density-sensing swarm · M6.1 (basal adenosine)

Same geometry as Setup 4a — a disk of \(N\) neutrophils in a petri dish of nondim radius \(\tilde{R}_{\text{dish}}\) — but every cell now also secretes a second diffusing species, adenosine \(\mathcal{A}\), at a constant basal rate (independent of the LTB4 cue). Adenosine does not gate emission; it raises the relay threshold \(1 \to 1 + \lambda_A\,\mathcal{A}\). Because the basal tone scales with local cell density, the threshold rises in step with production — the intended effect being to weaken the density dependence of the relay wave speed. Whether, and how strongly, that happens is measured by the density sweep below, not predicted here.

Why this model. M1–M4 all give a wave speed that grows with density. A density-dependent trigger-wave speed is the textbook driver of the streaming / channelisation instability (Höfer & Maini, Phys. Rev. E 56, 2074, 1997): a local density bump propagates the wave faster, cells chemotax in, density rises further. Real neutrophils do not channelise, and the measured relay speed is nearly density-independent (Strickland et al. 2024). M6.1 removes both symptoms with one mechanism.

Model M6.1 (basal-adenosine density sensor). The nondim equations (2D–2D) are:

\[ \partial_{\tilde{t}}\mathcal{L} = \tilde{\nabla}^2\mathcal{L} + \alpha\sum_i H^+\!\bigl(\mathcal{L}_i;\,1 + \lambda_A\,\mathcal{A}_i;\,n_L\bigr)\, \tilde{\delta}^2(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}-\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_i) - \Gamma_L\,\mathcal{L}, \qquad \alpha \equiv \frac{1}{\tilde{h}} = \frac{a}{h\,D_L\,L_0}. \]

The relay source prefactor \(\alpha=1/\tilde h\) is not a separate knob — it is fixed by the nondim layer height \(\tilde h = h/\ell_0\) (the \(\tilde h\) slider in the Geometry section, with \(\alpha\) shown live as a KPI). It sets the overall speed scale: \(c\propto\sqrt\alpha\).

\[ \partial_{\tilde{t}}\mathcal{A} = D\,\tilde{\nabla}^2\mathcal{A} + \sum_i \tilde{\delta}^2(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}-\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_i) - \tilde{\Gamma}_A\,\mathcal{A}, \qquad D = \frac{D_A}{D_L}. \]

where \(H^+(x;x_0;n) = x^n/(x_0^n+x^n)\) is the relay gate. The adenosine source is a bare \(\sum_i\tilde\delta^2\) — every cell emits unconditionally, with no \(H^+(\mathcal{L})\) factor. This is the key contrast with the M4 adenosine model, where production is LTB4-triggered. Absorbing BC \(\mathcal{L}=0\) at the dish edge; \(\mathcal{A}=0\) outside the dish (immaterial in the interior, since the screening length \(\tilde\ell_A=\sqrt{D/\tilde\Gamma_A}\ll \tilde{R}_{\text{dish}}\)).

Geometry (2D–2D vs 2D–3D). The equations above are the 2D–2D form (a thin well-mixed layer of height \(h\)). In 2D–3D both \(\mathcal{L}\) and \(\mathcal{A}\) diffuse in the 3D half-space above the cell floor and are solved on a direct thin-slab grid (explicit in-plane diffusion + Crank–Nicolson in \(z\)); cells sit at \(z=0\) and read \(\mathcal{A}|_{z=0}\). Two things change with geometry: the relay's own density scaling, and the basal tone, which becomes the screened surface value \(\mathcal{A}_{\text{ss}}=\tilde\sigma/\sqrt{D\,\tilde\Gamma_A}\) rather than \(\tilde\sigma/\tilde\Gamma_A\). Toggle Geometry and re-run the sweep to compare. Note 2D–3D is harder to ignite (the surface stimulus dilutes into the half-space and adenosine suppresses start-up), so the sweep uses a stronger kick; at high \(\lambda_A\) the low-density points may not ignite at all.

Symbol note. The model catalog calls the threshold-shift coupling \(\lambda\); here it is written \(\lambda_A\) to avoid a clash with the cell-side GL activation group \(\lambda = r_0 t_0 L_0\). Setting \(\lambda_A = 0\) recovers M1 exactly, so \(\lambda_A\) is a single dial from the bare relay (M1) to a fully density-adapted threshold.

What the mechanism does. The basal source gives a steady, spatially uniform adenosine tone present everywhere, including ahead of the front:

\[ \mathcal{A}_{\text{ss}} = \frac{\tilde\sigma}{\tilde\Gamma_A} \qquad\text{(2D–2D)}, \qquad \mathcal{A}_{\text{ss}} = \frac{\tilde\sigma}{\sqrt{D\,\tilde\Gamma_A}} \qquad\text{(2D–3D, at \(z=0\))}. \]

So the relay threshold each cell must cross, \(\mathcal{L}_c = 1 + \lambda_A\mathcal{A}_{\text{ss}}\), grows with density in step with production — the threshold and the drive move together instead of only the drive. That is the whole mechanism. We do not quote a closed-form \(c(\tilde\sigma)\) for it: the mean-field pushed-front flux balance does not describe this system reliably, so the density dependence is something to measure with the sweep below, not to fit against a formula. Suppressing channelisation additionally needs the screening length \(\tilde\ell_A=\sqrt{D/\tilde\Gamma_A}\) to be of order the relay length (\(\approx 1\)), so adenosine resolves local density bumps rather than acting as a global shift.

Limitation — no self-termination. Basal-only adenosine adapts the threshold but gives no arrest: cells behind the front keep firing, so the wave is a propagating / filling front, not a finite ring. For a finite radius as well, combine M6.1 (basal arm, ahead of the front) with a wake-accumulating M4-style inhibitor (triggered arm, behind it).

Length and time scales. Same intrinsic scales as M1:

\[ \ell_0 = \frac{a}{L_0\,D_L}, \qquad t_0 = \frac{\ell_0^2}{D_L} = \frac{a^2}{L_0^2\,D_L^3}. \]

GL cell SDE (Euler–Maruyama). Polarization \(\mathbf{P}_i\) and position \(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_i\) are unchanged from M1 — the threshold shift acts only on the relay emission gate, not on cell polarization:

\[ d\mathbf{P}_i = \Bigl[ \kappa\,\tilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L} + \lambda\,(\mathcal{L} - \mathcal{L}_c)\,\mathbf{P}_i + \nu\,(|\mathbf{P}_i|^2 - |\mathbf{P}_i|^4)\,\mathbf{P}_i \Bigr]d\tilde{t} + \sqrt{2\,\vartheta\,\mathcal{L}\,d\tilde{t}}\;\boldsymbol{\eta}_i, \] \[ \frac{d\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_i}{d\tilde{t}} = \mu\,\mathbf{P}_i. \]

Initial condition. As in M1, a time-limited firing source inside \(\tilde r < \tilde r_{\text{fire}}\) for \(\tilde t < \tilde t_{\text{fire}}\) nucleates the wave; the relay then self-sustains.

Try this. Run the density sweep at \(\lambda_A=0\) (the bare M1 relay), then repeat at increasing \(\lambda_A\) and compare how the measured points bend. The \(\mathcal{A}(\tilde r)\) panel shows the basal tone converging to \(\mathcal{A}_{\text{ss}}=\tilde\sigma/\tilde\Gamma_A\) (dashed line) — that part is an exact steady state of the linear \(\mathcal{A}\) equation and is worth checking.

Target — “stick to the target”. A circle of radius \(\tilde{R}_{\text{target}}\) at the dish centre stands for the object the swarm converges on (a candida cluster, a bead, a sterile injury). It is a one-way, absorbing inner boundary: the first time a cell reaches \(\tilde r_i \le \tilde{R}_{\text{target}}\) it is projected onto the circle and frozen there for the rest of the run — it has engaged the target. A stuck cell still polarizes and still emits the cue, so the cue model above is untouched; only \(d\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_i/d\tilde{t} = \mu\mathbf{P}_i\) is suppressed. The target disk is excluded from the initial cell placement, so no cell starts engaged. Switch it off in the controls to recover the plain dish (reflective outer wall only). Engaged cells are ringed in teal on the dish, and the on target KPI is the recruitment curve. See setup4_swarm3d.md §2c.

Interaction paradigm. Set all parameters, press Calculate, then scrub the time slider once the run finishes.

Numerical methods

Both \(\mathcal{L}\) and \(\mathcal{A}\) use explicit Euler for in-plane diffusion at CFL \(d\tilde{t}_{\text{sub}}\leq \Delta\tilde{x}^2/(4D)\) (with \(D=1\) for \(\mathcal{L}\)). Basal adenosine is smeared onto the grid by the same bilinear PIC weights as the LTB4 relay source; the emission threshold each cell sees is evaluated against the pre-step \(\mathcal{A}\). In 2D–3D both fields are advanced on a 3D thin-slab grid by the same generalized reaction–diffusion stepper (Crank–Nicolson in \(z\) on the non-uniform exponential grid), \(\mathcal{L}\) with \((D{=}1, \Gamma_L)\) and \(\mathcal{A}\) with \((D{=}D_A/D_L,\Gamma_A)\); the basal source enters as a \(\delta(z)\) surface flux at \(z=0\).

Channelisation \(\Psi(\tilde t)\) — does the swarm break into radial channels? Streaming shows up as structure in the angular distribution of cells, so it is measured from the azimuthal Fourier modes of the cell angles (cells outside a small excluded core, \(\tilde r > 0.1\tilde R_{\text{dish}}\)):

\[ c_m = \frac1N\sum_i e^{im\theta_i}, \qquad \Psi_m = \sqrt{\max\!\left(0,\ \frac{N|c_m|^2-1}{N-1}\right)} \ \in[0,1]. \]

The \(-1\) subtracts the shot-noise floor \(E[|c_m|^2]=1/N\) that even a perfectly uniform swarm produces, which is what makes \(\Psi\) comparable across densities: \(\Psi_m=0\) is angularly uniform, \(\Psi_m=1\) is every cell at one angle with \(m\)-fold periodicity. Left: \(\Psi=\max_{m\ge2}\Psi_m\) versus time — one number for "how channelised is it". Right: the whole spectrum \(\Psi_m\) at the scrubbed time; the dashed line is the 5% shot-noise level \(\sqrt{2/(N-1)}\) (bars below it mean nothing) and the highlighted dominant mode \(m^\star\) is the channel count — six spokes peak at \(m=6\). \(m=1\) is greyed out and excluded from \(\Psi\): it measures a bulk off-centre drift of the whole swarm, not spokes.

This is a cell-position diagnostic, identical on every Setup-4 page, so the comparison that matters is across pages at the same \(\tilde\sigma\): M1 is the channelising baseline the M6 variants are meant to suppress.

Density sweep \(\tilde c(\tilde\sigma)\). This is a multi-run diagnostic (its own Sweep c(σ̃) button in the controls, separate from Calculate): for each density it runs a short simulation and measures the relay front speed. The panel shows the measured points only — no analytical \(c(\tilde\sigma)\) curve is drawn, because the mean-field pushed-front flux balance does not describe this system reliably and plotting it only invited reading the simulation as a deviation from a law that was never right. Read the density dependence off the points: sweep once at \(\lambda_A=0\) for the bare-relay reference, then raise \(\lambda_A\) and sweep again, and compare the two shapes (the vertical marker shows where the single run on this page sits). The sweep uses a smaller dish/grid for speed; the measured points are a solver estimate.

Point colours encode why a point is what it is. Filled orange: a timed relay front. Filled red: no self-sustaining wave — the fired blob merely spreads diffusively. Hollow amber: a real but very slow front that did not clear the ignition halo within \(\tilde t_{\text{max}}\) — a window limit, not a zero. Hollow gray: the ignition stimulus alone would flood the dish, so nothing can be timed. The sweep tracks the emission front (where cells actually relay), keeps the injected \(\mathcal{L}\) mass fixed across densities (\(s_{\text{fire}}\propto1/\tilde\sigma\)) so the ignition halo has the same radius at every point, and starts timing only outside that halo — otherwise the stimulus spreading diffusively is mistimed as a wave. At large \(\lambda_A\) the relay may not ignite at all.

Bead-in-free-energy view. Pick a cell with the tracked cell slider — it appears as a large cyan crosshair on the dish above. The three panels below show its polarization \(P\) in the Ginzburg-Landau free energy with the chemotactic tilt:

\[ F(\mathbf{P}) = -\!\left[\lambda\,(\mathcal{L} - \mathcal{L}_c)\,\frac{|\mathbf{P}|^2}{2} + \nu\!\left(\frac{|\mathbf{P}|^4}{4} - \frac{|\mathbf{P}|^6}{6}\right)\right] \;-\; \kappa\;\nabla\mathcal{L}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{P}. \]

The last term tilts the otherwise rotationally-symmetric Mexican-hat well, lowering one side and raising the other. The yellow arrow in each panel points along \(\nabla\mathcal{L}\).
